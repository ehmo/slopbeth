# Voice and preservation

Slopbeth work starts with a preservation contract. Better rhythm is not a win if the rewrite drops a number, changes a claim, erases uncertainty, or turns the author into a generic professional.

## Preserve

- named entities, product names, places, people, organizations
- numbers, thresholds, dates, units, prices, percentages, versions
- URLs, citations, quotations, commands, identifiers, quoted terms
- causal claims and scope limits
- explicit uncertainty, probability, caveats, and modal verbs when they carry meaning
- first-person stance when the user chose it
- personal details, mixed feelings, dry humor, and deliberate unevenness

## Meaning checklist

For each paragraph, ask:

1. What claims does the source make?
2. Which facts make those claims verifiable or specific?
3. Which qualifiers keep the claim honest?
4. Did the rewrite add an unsupported claim?
5. Did the rewrite make a narrow claim broad, or a tentative claim certain?

Critical preservation failures block release: wrong number, changed entity, removed necessary qualifier, false causality, invented evidence, or omitted required constraint.

## Evidence boundary

Concrete detail is only an improvement when it is licensed by the source, by supplied context, or by an explicit user request to invent examples. Before adding specifics, label the source:

- `source`: directly present in the draft or supporting material
- `inference`: follows narrowly from the source without adding a new fact
- `placeholder`: useful example the user must verify
- `unsafe`: plausible detail that would read as fact but is not supported

For context-free rewrites, leave `unsafe` details out. Prefer sharper questions or bracketed placeholders over invented features, names, metrics, deadlines, owners, or workflows. In product or marketing copy, do not turn "workspace intelligence" or similar category language into asserted capabilities such as dashboards, status replacement, integrations, analytics, or outcomes unless source material supports them.

## Evidence-bound mode

Use evidence-bound mode when the draft is vague and no supporting context is present. The rewrite may improve rhythm, remove hype, tighten claims, or call out missing evidence, but it must not fill in the missing story.

Allowed moves:

- replace inflated claims with narrower claims already present in the source
- state that a claim needs proof instead of inventing the proof
- use bracketed slots such as `[specific deadline]`, `[owner]`, or `[customer-visible change]`
- ask for the facts needed to make the copy concrete
- offer a labeled hypothetical example only outside the final copy

Blocked moves:

- support replies: invented teams, outages, invoices, deadlines, amounts, or ownership
- recruiting: invented employers, projects, job levels, tools, or candidate strengths
- incidents: invented services, durations, failure modes, user impact, or remediation
- product copy: invented dashboards, tabs, integrations, analytics, automations, metrics, or outcomes
- strategy copy: invented quarterly plans, staffing moves, budget changes, owners, or governance rules

When evidence-bound mode would leave the text abstract, say that plainly. The correct Slopbeth output can be a critique plus a request for missing facts; density cannot come from fictional specificity.

## Voice checklist

Use author samples when available. Compare:

- sentence length and rhythm
- tolerance for bluntness, warmth, humor, or skepticism
- level of technical vocabulary
- amount of first-person framing
- typical specificity: scenes, names, examples, numbers, or sensory details
- acceptable rough edges

Do not "humanize" by injecting slang, typos, contractions, jokes, or confessions unless the sample supports them.

## Control samples

Already good human prose should often receive a light edit or no edit. Preserve:

- precise technical density
- formal but human academic prose
- non-native English choices that are clear and intentional
- lyrical or rhetorical passages where polish is part of the point

If the rewrite changes more than one fifth of a control sample without a clear reason, treat it as a warning.
